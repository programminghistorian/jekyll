# Issue 11 - July 2026

**Welcome** / **bienvenidos** / **bienvenue** / **bem-vindos** / to the eleventh issue of *Programming Historian*’s **bulletin** / **boletín** / **bulletin** / **boletim**. In this issue, we celebrate **Adam Crymble**, who stepped down from our Board of Trustees after many years of dedicated service, share news of editorial transitions across our journals, highlight new French translations, renew our call for volunteer peer reviewers, and look back on the achievements of **ENABLAR** as it draws to a close. We also extend our sincere thanks to our Institutional Partners and Patreons, whose continued support helps make our work possible.

## Honouring Adam Crymble's contributions 

This quarter, we honour the service and commitment of Adam Crymble, who has served on the Board since 2011 – and who, this June, stepped down from their role as Trustee. Thank you, Adam, for everything you've given this organisation, and for everything you've given so many of us individually.

**Maria José Afandor-Llach**, **James Baker**, **Riva Quiroga** and **Anisa Hawes** share their tributes. 

>Adam brought together a community of Digital Humanities practitioners into a global-scale thriving editorial project in which many of us learned and collaborated. I'm grateful for his mentorship, openness, and support when we started with _Programming Historian en español_ in 2016. I will never forget our Bogota's workshop. Gracias Adam.  
>
>Maria José Afandor-Llach

>For me - and for I expect many others - Adam Crymble and _Programming Historian_ are indivisible. Adam has now stepped down after 16 years of service and this marks an inflection point for the project. One of Adam's great qualities is that when he does something, he does it well. His list of achievements whilst working on the _Programming Historian_ back that up. He was part of the team that launched _Programming Historian_ as an open access peer reviewed journal. He [championed diversity when that diversity was lacking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mDyzpCQs6dk). He won a [British Academy grant](https://zenodo.org/records/1473414) that created the foundations for the _Programming Historian en español_. And when _Programming Historian_ incorporated first as a company and latterly as a charity, Adam was its founding Chair of Directors and Trustees, guiding us through those initial years of organisational growth. Adam's attention to detail, challenge mindset, and deep institutional knowledge of the project are qualities that I - and suspect many in the project team - will miss deeply. Thank you Adam for all you've done for the _Programming Historian_ and for the wider community. Your work has changed the digital humanities for the better.
>
>James Baker

>I first met Adam at the Bogotá workshop, an experience made possible by his work and leadership. Looking back, I can see that it was, in many ways, a turning point for me. It opened the door to a community that has profoundly shaped both my professional and personal life, and to a project that showed me what genuine collaboration could look like. Adam believed that _Programming Historian_ could become something bigger than an English-language publication, and he was deeply committed to making that vision a reality. His allyship meant that many of us felt empowered to contribute and to lead, and to imagine _Programming Historian_ as a project we could truly call our own.
Thanks to the community he helped build, many of us found collaborators, friendships, and a sense of belonging. For that, I will always be grateful. ¡Muchas gracias, Adam!
>
>Riva Quiroga

>From my very first day in 2021, Adam’s wholehearted commitment to and investment in _Programming Historian_ were absolutely clear. He gave me the opportunity and encouragement to develop the skills needed to support our publications and to build strong relationships across our teams. Adam has been my mentor and my advocate, making it possible for me to grow within this very special organisation. I will always be grateful for his generosity, guidance, and belief in me.
>
>Anisa Hawes

## New Translations

- Jonathan Blaney, traduction par David Valentine  
  [Introduction aux principes des données ouvertes liées](https://doi.org/10.46430/phfr0042)
  
  - Cette leçon présente les concepts fondamentaux des données ouvertes liées (Linked Open Data), incluant les URI, les ontologies, les formats de données RDF et une introduction en douceur au langage de requête SPARQL.

- Nicolás Vaughan, traduction par Maritza Beatriz García Rodríguez  
  [Introduction à l'encodage de texte TEI (partie 1)](https://doi.org/10.46430/phfr0040)
  
  - Cette leçon (qui sera suivie d’une deuxième partie) vous enseigne les rudiments de la TEI-XML pour encoder des textes.

- Daniel van Strien, Kaspar Beelen, Melvin Wevers, Thomas Smits, et Katherine McDonough, traduction par Jean-Philippe Moreux et Matthias Gille Levenson  
  [La vision par ordinateur pour les sciences humaines : introduction à la classification d'images par apprentissage profond (partie 1)](https://doi.org/10.46430/phfr0041)
  
  - Ceci est le premier volet d’une leçon en deux parties qui présente des méthodes de vision par ordinateur basées sur l’apprentissage profond pour la recherche en sciences humaines. À partir d’un ensemble de publicités extraites de journaux historiques, la leçon explique comment entrainer un modèle de vision par ordinateur pour la classification d’images avec la bibliothèque Python fastai.

- Gabriel Calarco et Gimena del Río Riande, traduction par Yanet Hernández Pedraza  
  [Introduction à la publication web de fichiers TEI avec CETEIcean](https://doi.org/10.46430/phfr0039)
 
  - Cette leçon vous enseigne les étapes nécessaires pour publier en ligne un fichier TEI en utilisant CETEIcean.

## Renewing our Call for Reviewers

**Would you like to contribute to the development of another high-quality *Programming Historian* lesson?** We’re seeking volunteers who are available within the next 12 months to review lessons in any of our four languages. Reviewing for *Programming Historian* is an opportunity to learn new technical skills and engage with the digital humanities community.

**Who can participate?** Anyone who is working, teaching, or learning with computational methods. You might be an educator, a researcher, a PhD candidate, a Research Software Engineer, a librarian, a linguist, a historian – if you share our interest in using digital methods to acquire, transform, analyse, present or preserve data, we’d love to hear from you.

## ENABLAR update

As [**ENABLAR**](/posts/enablar-call-for-participants) draws to a close this month, it's a good moment to reflect on what the project has achieved. Funded by **[Jisc](https://www.jisc.ac.uk/)**, **ENABLAR** has brought together a cross-disciplinary cohort to co-author lessons in computational methods for library and archive professionals, supporting digital transformation through skills development and knowledge sharing.

The response to our call for participants exceeded expectations, and our cohort spans the UK and Ireland, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, and the US, bringing together expertise from libraries, archives, and the digital humanities.

Together, the cohort has developed four practical lessons, each rooted in a real challenge facing library and archive professionals: understanding AI licence agreements, exploring catalogues as data, visualising connections across collections, and helping researchers discover people across large archival collections. 

This July, we tested the lessons through a series of hands-on workshops. Each session gave participants the chance to try out the methods for themselves and explore how they could apply them in their own work. The workshops also provided feedback ahead of peer review and helped us connect with potential reviewers from across the community.

Alongside the lessons, we're also developing guidance on using _Programming Historian_ lessons in continuing professional development (CPD), drawing on feedback from the cohort to ensure it reflects real teaching and learning experiences.

A huge thank you to our cohort for their creativity and hard work, to everyone who joined our workshops and shared their feedback, and to **Jisc** for making the project possible. We're excited to share the finished lessons with the wider community once they've completed peer review, and we hope they'll become a useful resource for library and archive professionals who want to develop computational skills in their own practice.
